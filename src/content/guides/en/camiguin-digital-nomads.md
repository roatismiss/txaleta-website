# Camiguin for Digital Nomads: The Honest Version (2026)

**We are Txaleta de Camiguin, a small resort on the island's east-facing cliffs, and we would very much like your month-long booking — so read this knowing that, and then notice how much of it tells you not to come.** Camiguin is not a digital-nomad base. It has no café scene built for laptops, no community of remote workers to fall into, and an internet connection that is real but modest. We have said as much in our [Camiguin vs Siargao comparison](/guides/camiguin-vs-siargao), and we will not unsay it here to win a booking.

What Camiguin *is* is one of the best places in the Philippines for a particular kind of working trip: **two to four weeks of focused, mostly asynchronous work**, with mornings on a volcano and the sea, and a time zone that suits Europe surprisingly well. This page is about telling those two things apart — and about how to make an island connection reliable enough to trust with a deadline.

![The shared lounge at Txaleta de Camiguin: a long wooden table, glass doors onto the terrace and late light across the floor](/images/resort/common-area.webp)

> **Quick answer:** Camiguin works for remote workers whose job is **mostly writing, coding, designing or thinking**, with a few video calls a week — and badly for anyone whose day is back-to-back calls, live streaming or large daily uploads. Wi-Fi at resorts and the better guesthouses is **good enough for messages, documents and a video call you do not mind dropping once**; ours comes in over **Starlink**, with the router on our generator. Make it dependable by carrying **mobile data on both Globe and Smart** as a backup. The island runs on **UTC+8**, which puts a **European working day in your afternoon and evening** and leaves the island's best hours — the mornings — free. Most Western passports enter **visa-free for 30 days**, extendable with the Bureau of Immigration. If you need a café full of other nomads, go to **Siargao**; if you need fibre and coworking, **Cebu City**.

*Written by the family at Txaleta de Camiguin, on an east-facing clifftop above the Bohol Sea. Published November 2026.*

## Is Camiguin good for digital nomads?

**For most digital nomads, no. For some, it is close to ideal.** The difference is almost entirely the shape of your working day.

| | **Camiguin works if…** | **Go elsewhere if…** |
|---|---|---|
| **Your work** | Is writing, code, design, analysis, a thesis — output you produce alone | Is calls, sales, support, teaching, live streaming |
| **Video calls** | A handful a week, and a dropped one is an annoyance, not a lost client | Several a day, with clients who judge you by the connection |
| **Uploads** | Documents and the occasional file | Raw video, large photo sets, daily heavy syncs |
| **Your clients** | Are in Europe, or are async across time zones | Are on US hours and expect you live in theirs |
| **Your evenings** | Are for quiet, books and the stars | Are for meeting people |
| **Your stay** | Two to four weeks, with a deadline you want to hit | Open-ended, with a social life to build |
| **Better base instead** | — | Siargao for community, Cebu City for infrastructure |

If you are reading the left-hand column and recognising yourself, keep going. If you are in the right-hand one, the rest of this page is still useful for a holiday from your work — but do not plan to *do* the work here.

## The internet, honestly

**Wi-Fi on Camiguin is real but modest.** Resorts, hotels and the better guesthouses and restaurants have it. It is good enough for email, messaging, documents, cloud tools, a code repository and video calls — most of the time. It is not the fibre you would get in a Cebu or Manila office building, and it is not something to bet a client relationship on without a backup. That is the same thing our [practical travel tips](/guides/camiguin-travel-tips) say, and it is the honest summary for the island as a whole.

**Ours comes down from Starlink, and our generator runs the router.** It reaches the rooms and the public areas, a power cut on the island does not take it with it, and because it does not depend on the island's cables and mobile towers, it fails for different reasons from everything else on Camiguin — which, as the next section explains, is exactly what you want from a connection. What a satellite link does do is slow, or drop for a few minutes, under very heavy rain. Every one of them makes that trade, and it is why the advice below about a second connection applies to us too.

We are not going to print a speed figure on this page, and that is a decision rather than an evasion: a number on a website is one test on one day, and the thing that matters to you is what the connection does from the room you are booking, at the hour you will be working, this month. **Message us before you book and ask for a speed test from that room** — upload as well as download, ideally taken in the evening. Any place that wants remote workers should be able to send you a screenshot, and that includes us. If the answer does not suit your work, it is better for both of us that you find out then.

**Ask about the upload, not the download.** Download speed is the number every listing quotes and the one that matters least on a call. What drops a video call is the *upload* — your camera and voice going out — and, above all, its *stability*. A connection that holds a modest, steady speed beats one that touches a high number and then stutters. If you are asking any place to test for you, ask for upload and ask for two or three readings at different times.

**Mobile data is your second connection, not your first.** Both Philippine networks, **Globe and Smart**, cover Camiguin. Signal is reliable along the populated ring road where almost everything is, and patchy inland and up the volcano. A phone hotspot will carry a video call perfectly well where the signal is good, and an hour of HD video can easily use a gigabyte or more, so buy data accordingly. The SIM registration rule — every prepaid SIM must be registered to your passport before it works — is explained in the [travel tips guide](/guides/camiguin-travel-tips); do it at the airport on the mainland, or arrive on a travel eSIM.

## Two connections do not halve your downtime — they cut it twentyfold

This is the one idea on this page worth taking to any island, anywhere, and almost nobody explains it properly.

**Reliability multiplies when the failures are independent.** Suppose your resort Wi-Fi is up 95% of the time, which sounds decent. Over a 30-day month that is **36 hours without a connection** — and those hours will not politely arrange themselves around your calls.

Now add a mobile-data hotspot that is also, independently, up 95% of the time. You are only offline when *both* are down at once. The chance of that is 5% of 5%: **0.25%**. Over the same month, that is **under two hours** — roughly twenty times better, from a second connection that costs you a SIM card.

**The catch is the word "independently."** Two connections that fail for the same reason are one connection. On a small island, the common causes are:

- **A power cut.** It takes down the router unless the router is on backup power — ours is on the generator, but ask wherever else you stay. Mobile towers generally carry batteries for some hours, so your phone hotspot usually survives an outage the Wi-Fi does not — which is precisely why it is the right backup.
- **A storm.** Heavy weather can affect both at once: very heavy rain slows any satellite link, Starlink included, and a typhoon can take out towers. This is rare, and it is also the day your client will remember.
- **One mobile network.** A Globe SIM backing up a router that is itself fed over Globe is not as independent as it looks. Ours is fed by satellite, so either network is a genuine second connection here; anywhere else, ask what feeds the router. **Carry one SIM from each network** and you have three connections with three different points of failure.

**The test you can run anywhere — pull the plug.** Before your first important call, switch off Wi-Fi on your laptop and join a test call through your phone's hotspot. If the call works, you have a backup. If it does not, you have a hope. It takes five minutes, and it is the single most useful thing a remote worker can do on arrival anywhere that is not a city.

## Power: what happens when it goes

**Brief power cuts happen on Camiguin**, as they do on any small island, usually after heavy weather. Most are short. None of them should be able to end your working day if you have planned for one.

- **Keep the laptop charged** rather than running it off the wall all day. A full battery is a three- to eight-hour uninterruptible power supply that you already own.
- **Carry a power bank** big enough to keep your phone — and therefore your hotspot — going for a long call.
- **Ask whether there is a generator, and what it runs.** "We have a generator" can mean the kitchen fridges and nothing else. The question that matters to you is whether it powers the router. Ask everywhere. Our own answer is yes — the generator runs the router, so the Wi-Fi stays up through a cut. Our [where to stay guide](/guides/where-to-stay-in-camiguin) puts both questions — backup power and a Wi-Fi number rather than a yes — on its checklist for exactly this reason.

## The time zone is the real advantage

Camiguin is on **Philippine time, UTC+8, all year** — no daylight saving. It is the same clock as Singapore, Hong Kong and Perth, one hour behind Tokyo and Seoul. And it decides more about whether a working trip here succeeds than the Wi-Fi does.

| Your clients are in… | Their 9 to 5 is, on Camiguin… | What it does to your island day |
|---|---|---|
| **UK** | 4 pm–midnight (British summer) · 5 pm–1 am (winter) | Mornings entirely free; a late finish |
| **Central Europe** | 3–11 pm (summer) · 4 pm–midnight (winter) | The best fit of any region: island morning, working evening |
| **US East Coast** | 9 pm–5 am (summer) · 10 pm–6 am (winter) | A night shift. Overlap only by staying up |
| **US West Coast** | Midnight–8 am (summer) · 1–9 am (winter) | The worst fit. Async only |
| **Sydney / Melbourne** | 7 am–3 pm (their winter) · 6 am–2 pm (their summer) | You work through the island's best hours |
| **Singapore / Hong Kong / Perth** | 9 am–5 pm — the same | An ordinary office day, with the afternoon heat to sit out |

**Why Europe fits so well.** Camiguin's best hours are its earliest: sunrise over the sea, an empty sandbar at six, the volcano before the summit clouds over. By the time a European client's day starts at three or four in the afternoon, the island's heat has peaked and you would be indoors anyway. **You trade a late evening for a morning nobody else on your team will ever have.** For many people that is the whole point of coming.

**Why the US does not.** Overlap with an American working day lands between nine at night and the early morning. You can do it for a week. Over a month it eats the mornings that make the island worth the trip, because you will be asleep through them.

## A working day that actually works here

This is the rhythm we see work for guests who are here to get something done, built for a European client day. Adjust the evening to your own overlap.

- **5:30** — Sunrise over the Bohol Sea. On an east-facing coast it comes up over the water every morning of the year; you will not need an alarm after the first week.
- **6:00–8:00** — The island's hours. A swim, a run on the ring road, the sandbar, a waterfall before the tour vans, or the first half of a volcano.
- **8:00** — Breakfast.
- **9:00–12:00** — **Deep work.** The quiet block: writing, code, the hard problem. No calls exist yet, because Europe is asleep.
- **12:00–15:00** — The heat. A long lunch, a sleep, the cold spring, a book. Do not try to fight this; nobody on the island does.
- **15:00–19:00** — **Collaboration.** Europe wakes; calls and messages land. Do your synchronous work here, with the backup hotspot ready.
- **19:00** — Dinner, the stars, and the laptop closed. A hard stop is easier on an island with nothing to do after dark — which is a feature, if you came to finish something.

**Plan the outings, not the work.** Boats leave at dawn and are cancelled by weather, so protect one flexible morning a week for the sandbar or [Mantigue Island](/guides/mantigue-island-camiguin), and keep the [Hibok-Hibok climb](/guides/hibok-hibok-hike) for a day with no calls at all — you will not want to be on one afterwards.

## Visas and staying longer

**Immigration rules change, and we are a resort, not immigration lawyers.** Treat this as orientation and confirm the current position with the Bureau of Immigration or a Philippine embassy before you plan around it.

- **Visa-free entry.** Most Western passports, including those of the UK, the EU, the US, Canada and Australia, can enter the Philippines visa-free for **30 days**. Check your own nationality's rules; some get less, and some need a visa in advance.
- **Extensions.** Stays can be extended through the **Bureau of Immigration**, in instalments, well beyond the first month. Many extensions can now be filed online. There is also a BI field office on Camiguin, reported at the Provincial Capitol in Mambajao — confirm what it currently processes before you ride over, and do not leave an extension to your last day.
- **The digital nomad visa.** An executive order signed in **April 2025** created a Philippine digital nomad visa. As we write, in November 2026, reports conflict on whether it is actually being issued, and the income threshold and fees have not been reliably published. **Check with the Philippine embassy in your country**; do not rely on a figure you read on a blog, including this one.

**Tax and the legality of working on a tourist stay** depend on your nationality, your employer and how long you stay. Take proper advice if your trip is long enough for it to matter.

Camiguin is inexpensive by international standards, and a working month is cheaper than a holiday month, because you eat out less and go on fewer boats. These figures come from our [Camiguin travel cost guide](/guides/camiguin-travel-cost); prices drift, so treat them as orientation.

## The honest bit

Every section above is the case for working here. This is the case against, and it is longer than you might expect from a resort.

**There is no nomad scene.** We do not know of a dedicated coworking space on Camiguin. The cafés in Mambajao are pleasant and are not built for four hours with a laptop. If being around other remote workers is part of what keeps you productive — or keeps you sane over a long stay — this island will feel lonely after the first week. Siargao has that community; we do not, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

**The connection is good, not great.** Everything in the sections above is about making a modest connection dependable. None of it turns it into fibre. If your work needs consistently fast uploads, Camiguin is the wrong island.

**One flight a day.** The only nonstop flight is the daily Cebgo turboprop from Cebu, and when it is full or cancelled, the alternative is a long overland chain through Cagayan de Oro. If your work might call you to a meeting in Manila at a day's notice, factor that in.

**Evenings are quiet.** There is no nightlife to speak of, which is wonderful for finishing a book and less wonderful in week three if you came alone.

**And we are seven kilometres from town.** On our cliff it is quiet because nothing is built along this coast, which is why people come — and it means groceries, the pharmacies and the ATMs are a drive. You will want a scooter or a driver for a working month here; there is no ride-hailing on the island, which our [getting-around guide](/guides/getting-around-camiguin) explains.

## Better Philippine bases, if Camiguin is not yours

We would rather send you somewhere that fits.

- **Siargao** — the Philippines' surf island and its best-known nomad base. Cafés with working Wi-Fi, a large community of remote workers, coliving, and a social life that absorbs newcomers. Our [Camiguin vs Siargao guide](/guides/camiguin-vs-siargao) credits it properly.
- **Cebu City** — the big-city option. Coworking spaces, faster fixed connections, malls, hospitals and the country's second hub airport. It is a city, not an island idyll, and that is the point.
- **Dumaguete** — a university city on Negros with an established expat community, cafés and a slower pace than Cebu, close to diving at Apo Island.

**The arrangement we suggest most often:** base yourself in Cebu or Siargao for the call-heavy weeks, and come to Camiguin for a focused **two-week sprint** — the chapter, the release, the proposal — when what you need is the opposite of a scene. Cebu to Camiguin is 55 minutes in the air.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Camiguin good for digital nomads?
For most digital nomads, no: there is no café scene built for laptops, no community of remote workers and the internet is modest rather than fast. For people whose work is mostly writing, coding, design or analysis, with a few video calls a week, it can be excellent for a focused stay of two to four weeks, particularly with European clients, whose working day falls in Camiguin's afternoon and evening.

### Is the internet in Camiguin good enough for remote work?
For most document, messaging and cloud work, yes. Resorts, hotels and the better guesthouses have Wi-Fi that handles email, shared documents and video calls most of the time; ours at Txaleta de Camiguin runs on Starlink, with the router on the generator. None of it should be your only connection. Carry mobile data on Globe and Smart as a backup, ask any place for a speed test from your actual room, including upload, and test your backup before your first important call.

### Can you do video calls from Camiguin?
Yes, a few a week, provided you have a backup. Video calls depend on upload speed and stability rather than download, so ask for upload figures before booking. A phone hotspot on Globe or Smart carries a call well wherever the signal is good, which is most of the populated ring road. An hour of HD video can use a gigabyte or more of data, so buy mobile data accordingly.

### How do you make a remote-work connection reliable on an island?
Use two or three independent connections. If your Wi-Fi is up 95 percent of the time, you lose about 36 hours a month; add a mobile hotspot that is independently up 95 percent of the time and you are only offline when both fail together, about 0.25 percent of the time, or under two hours a month. Use a different mobile network from the one feeding the router, and test the hotspot before you need it.

### Should you carry two SIM cards to work from Camiguin?
Yes, one on Globe and one on Smart. Both networks cover Camiguin, with reliable signal along the populated ring road and patchy coverage inland. Two networks mean two sets of towers, so a problem on one rarely affects the other. Every Philippine SIM must be registered to your passport before it activates, so buy and register them at an official airport counter on the mainland, or use a travel eSIM for one of them.

### Are there coworking spaces in Camiguin?
We do not know of a dedicated coworking space on the island, and the cafés in Mambajao are not set up for long laptop sessions. Remote workers on Camiguin generally work from where they are staying, so it matters to choose a room with a proper desk, air conditioning and a tested connection. If you want coworking and a community of other remote workers, Siargao or Cebu City suit you better.

### What time zone is Camiguin in, and does it suit remote work?
Camiguin uses Philippine time, UTC+8, with no daylight saving. That puts a European working day in the afternoon and evening, from about 3 or 4 pm, which leaves the island's best hours, the mornings, free. A US working day falls overnight, which is a poor fit for more than a week. Australian and East Asian hours line up with the local morning and early afternoon.

### Does the Philippines have a digital nomad visa?
An executive order signed in April 2025 created a Philippine digital nomad visa. As of November 2026, reports conflict on whether it is actually being issued, and the income threshold and fees have not been reliably published. Check with the Philippine embassy in your country before planning around it. Most remote workers on shorter stays enter visa-free instead and extend through the Bureau of Immigration.

### How long can you stay in the Philippines without a visa?
Most Western passports, including those of the UK, EU, US, Canada and Australia, can enter visa-free for 30 days. That stay can be extended through the Bureau of Immigration in instalments, many of them online, and the Bureau has a field office on Camiguin, reported at the Provincial Capitol in Mambajao. Rules and fees change and vary by nationality, so confirm the current position before you travel.

### How much does a working month in Camiguin cost?
Much less than a holiday month, because you eat out less and take fewer boats. Guesthouses run about 1,500 to 2,800 pesos a night, usually less on a monthly arrangement; a scooter is about 450 pesos a day; a local eatery meal 80 to 150 pesos and a casual restaurant main 250 to 450. Add two mobile data plans, travel insurance with medical evacuation and the flight from Cebu.

### Are there power cuts in Camiguin?
Brief ones happen, as on any small island, usually after heavy weather, and most are short. Keep your laptop charged rather than running it from the wall, carry a power bank big enough to run your phone hotspot through a long call, and ask wherever you stay whether the generator powers the router, not just the kitchen; at Txaleta de Camiguin it does. Mobile towers generally have battery backup, which is why a hotspot is the right backup connection.

### Where is better than Camiguin for digital nomads in the Philippines?
Siargao is the best-known nomad base, with working cafés, coliving and a large remote-work community. Cebu City has coworking spaces, faster fixed connections and the country's second hub airport. Dumaguete is a slower university city with an expat community. A good pattern is to base yourself in one of those for call-heavy weeks and come to Camiguin for a focused two-week sprint.

### Which room should a remote worker book on Camiguin?
Wherever you stay, look for a proper desk and chair, air conditioning for the afternoon heat, a tested connection with upload figures, and backup power for the router. At Txaleta de Camiguin that is the Premier Seaview Suite, which has a work desk, air conditioning and a terrace over the sea. The glamping domes are air-conditioned but built for sleeping, not for a working day.
