# Camiguin Travel Tips: Money, SIMs, Water and Wi-Fi (2026)

Every guide to this island will tell you about the sandbar and the sunken cemetery. Almost none of them will tell you that **the ATM queue in Mambajao on a Friday before a long weekend is a real thing**, that your SIM card has to be registered to your passport before it works, or that the water coming out of the tap is not the water you should be drinking.

These are the questions our guests actually message us about, usually two days before they fly. So this is the unglamorous page: money, signal, water, power, medicine, and what is open on a Sunday. Everything here is first-hand, and everything that moves — a price, a schedule, a rule — is flagged as something to re-check rather than trusted from an article.

![A table set outdoors on the Txaleta clifftop above the Bohol Sea, the small practical comforts of a day on Camiguin](/images/resort/txaleta_picnic.webp)

> **Quick answer:** **Bring cash.** Camiguin has few ATMs, all of them around Mambajao, and almost everything outside resorts and hotels is cash-only — fares, entrance fees, stalls, carinderias. **Buy a Globe or Smart SIM on the mainland** and know that Philippine law requires it to be registered to your passport before activation, which is easier done at an airport counter than on an island. **Do not drink the tap water** — purified refill water is everywhere and costs almost nothing. Power is **220V, 60Hz**, with flat two-pin sockets. The provincial hospital is in Mambajao, serious cases are flown out, and **travel insurance with medical evacuation is the one thing we would not skip.** Sunday mornings the island goes to church, and much of it closes.

*Written by the family at Txaleta de Camiguin, on the Mambajao clifftop. Published October 2026.*

## Money: bring more cash than feels sensible

This is the single most useful line in this guide. **Camiguin runs on cash.**

**ATMs exist, and they are concentrated around Mambajao**, the capital on the north coast. There are a small number of them, they are attached to the banks in town rather than scattered around the ring road, and they have the two failure modes every island ATM has: out of cash after a weekend or a holiday, and offline when the connection drops. Neither is common. Both are inconvenient enough that you should not plan a day around needing one.

**What to do instead, which is what we tell every guest:**

- **Withdraw in Cebu, Cagayan de Oro or Manila before you fly in.** Mainland machines are more numerous, better stocked, and usually have higher per-withdrawal limits.
- **Bring small denominations.** A ₱1,000 note is awkward for a ₱50 tricycle fare, a ₱75 waterfall entrance or a bag of lanzones from a roadside stall. Ask for ₱100s and ₱50s when you withdraw, and break big notes at supermarkets rather than at stalls.
- **Expect card acceptance to be thin.** Resorts and hotels take cards. Most restaurants outside them, every carinderia, every tricycle, every entrance gate and every boatman does not.
- **Budget the fees.** Philippine ATMs typically charge a withdrawal fee on top of whatever your own bank adds, so **fewer, larger withdrawals cost less than many small ones** — which argues again for arriving with cash in hand.

**How much?** Our [full cost breakdown](/guides/camiguin-travel-cost) has the real numbers, but as a rule of thumb: all the entrance fees for a whole day of the island's greatest hits come to under ₱300 per person, a scooter is ₱450 a day, and boats are ₱550 to ₱800 per boat rather than per person. You need less than you think — you just need it in notes.

## SIM cards, data and the registration rule nobody mentions

**Buy your SIM on the mainland, at the airport, before you fly to Camiguin.** This is the practical version of a legal fact.

**The two networks are Globe and Smart**, and both cover Camiguin. Coverage is reliable along the populated northern ring road and patchy inland, up the volcano flanks and in the folds of the interior — the same pattern we describe in our guide to [getting around the island](/guides/getting-around-camiguin). For a traveller, either network is fine; locals will have opinions.

**The rule that catches people out: since the SIM Registration Act, every prepaid SIM in the Philippines has to be registered to an identity document before it will work.** For a visitor that means your passport, and it means the registration step is not optional and not instant. Airport kiosks and official stores handle it for you in a few minutes. A small shop on an island may not be set up to, and then you are standing there with a SIM that will not activate.

**Three ways to arrive connected:**

1. **An official counter at Cebu, Manila or Cagayan de Oro airport.** They register it with your passport on the spot. Simplest route.
2. **A travel eSIM**, bought before you leave home and activated on landing. This sidesteps the physical-SIM registration question entirely and is what an increasing number of our guests do. Check your phone supports eSIM first.
3. **Roaming**, which works and costs what roaming costs.

**Wi-Fi on the island** is real but modest. Resorts, hotels and the better restaurants have it; expect it to be good enough for messages, maps and a video call that you do not mind dropping once, rather than for uploading a day's photographs. Ours reaches the rooms and the public areas. If your trip depends on being reliably online, bring mobile data as the backup rather than the other way round.

## Water: do not drink from the tap

**Tap water on Camiguin is not for drinking.** It is fine for showering and brushing teeth, which is what almost everyone does, and we are not aware of guests having trouble with that.

**What everyone here actually drinks is purified refill water**, sold from refilling stations by the litre or in the big blue containers you will see everywhere. It costs very little. Resorts and hotels provide drinking water — we put it in the rooms — and restaurants serve purified water as a matter of course.

**Practical version:** bring a refillable bottle, fill it from the dispenser wherever you are staying, and buy bottled only when you are caught out. A sandbar morning with no shade needs more water than you think; so does a waterfall day on a scooter.

**Ice and fresh produce** are not something we would worry about in restaurants or resorts here. Use the judgement you would use anywhere: busy places with turnover are a better bet than empty ones.

## Power, plugs and the one thing to pack

**220 volts, 60 hertz.** The sockets are the flat two-pin type (A), with some taking the flat-pins-plus-round-earth type (B) and a few older round two-pin (C) fittings still around. If you are coming from the UK, Europe or Australia you need an adapter; from North America you need the adapter less than you need to check your device handles 220V, though almost all modern chargers do.

**Bring a power bank.** Not for power cuts — though brief ones happen on any small island, usually after weather — but because a day out here is long, your phone is also your camera and your map, and there is no café socket at a waterfall.

## Medical: the honest version

**The provincial hospital is in Mambajao**, there are pharmacies in town, and for ordinary problems — a cut, a stomach upset, an infected coral scrape — you are perfectly well looked after on the island.

**For anything serious, the answer is a flight.** Camiguin is a small island province; definitive care for a major injury or illness means getting to Cagayan de Oro or Cebu. That is not alarming, it is simply the geography, and it is exactly why we say the same thing in our [guide to safety in the Philippines](/guides/is-the-philippines-safe) and our [cost breakdown](/guides/camiguin-travel-cost):

**Buy travel insurance that explicitly covers medical evacuation, and read that section of the policy.** It is the single highest-value thing on this page. Everything else here costs you a small inconvenience if you get it wrong.

**Bring your own small kit.** Reef-safe sunscreen (the sun here is closer to the equator than most people's instincts allow for), insect repellent, rehydration salts, plasters, motion-sickness tablets if bancas bother you, and anything prescription in its original packaging with enough for the whole trip plus a few days. Specific brands you rely on may not be stocked.

**The emergency number is 911**, which works nationwide.

## What is open, and when

This catches more visitors than any other item on this page.

**Sunday morning, the island goes to church.** Camiguin is deeply Catholic, the mass schedule structures the day, and a good deal of ordinary commerce simply is not happening while it does. Plan Sunday mornings for the beach, the springs or a slow breakfast rather than for errands.

**Markets are morning things.** Mambajao's public market is at its best early; by afternoon the best of it is gone. Our guide to [Mambajao town](/guides/mambajao-camiguin) has the detail.

**Restaurants close earlier than you expect, and some close entirely on quiet days.** This island is not a dining scene, which we say plainly in our [restaurant guide](/guides/best-restaurant-in-camiguin) — **call ahead before you drive**, because twenty kilometres of unlit ring road to a closed gate is a memorable evening for the wrong reasons.

**Holidays compress everything.** Holy Week and the days around Christmas and New Year are when Filipinos travel domestically; the island fills, boats and flights sell out, and ATMs run dry. The [month-by-month guide](/guides/best-time-to-visit-camiguin) marks which weeks to book early or avoid, and the [Lanzones Festival](/guides/camiguin-lanzones-festival) in the second half of October is the one week this quiet island genuinely is not quiet.

## Fees, tickets and how the small payments work

A pattern worth understanding, because it recurs everywhere:

- **Entrance fees are per person, small, and paid in cash at a gate.** Tens of pesos, not hundreds.
- **Boat fares are per boat, not per person.** This makes the islands and the sandbar dramatically cheaper for four people than for one.
- **Environmental fees** are separate from boat fares and charged per person at the jump-off. White Island and Mantigue both work this way.
- **Guides are mandatory for some things.** The [Mt. Hibok-Hibok climb](/guides/hibok-hibok-hike) requires a permit and an accredited guide; so does [birdwatching inside the protected forest](/guides/birdwatching-camiguin-hibok-hibok). Arrange these in advance, not at the trailhead.
- **Nobody issues a receipt you will want to keep**, and that is normal. Agree the number before, not after.

**The provincial tourism office** in Mambajao is the right authority for current fees, festival schedules and guide accreditation. When a number on this site matters to your budget, that office — or us, if you are staying with us — is a better source than any article, including this one.

## Tipping, etiquette and a few words of Bisaya

**Tipping is appreciated and not obligatory.** Many restaurants, ours included, add a service charge of around 5% that goes to the team; beyond that, rounding up a fare or leaving something for housekeeping is normal and welcome rather than expected.

**The language of the island is Bisaya (Cebuano).** English is widely spoken and you will have no practical difficulty anywhere. But four words go a surprisingly long way:

- **Maayong buntag** — good morning
- **Salamat** — thank you (*daghang salamat*, thank you very much)
- **Pila?** — how much?
- **Walay problema** — no problem

**A note on photographs.** Ask before photographing people, particularly children and particularly at the market. This is a small island; you may well see the same person tomorrow.

**And on the environment, since we live with the consequences:** reef-safe sunscreen matters where the coral is ten metres from shore, nothing stands on the giant clams, and whatever you carry to White Island comes back with you. There are no bins on a sandbar.

## The things we wish people knew before arriving

**There is no ride-hailing on this island at all.** No Grab, no Uber. Arrange your airport or port pickup before you land — the [transport guide](/guides/getting-around-camiguin) covers every option.

**The island has two ports and people mix them up.** Benoni on the south-east takes the Cagayan de Oro ferries; Balbagon near Mambajao takes the Bohol ferries. They are the better part of an hour apart.

**Sunrise is the hour that matters here.** White Island has no shade whatsoever and is punishing by nine. Katibawasan Falls fills with tour vans by mid-morning. The island rewards early starts more than almost anywhere we know.

**Three days is the minimum that works, five is the number people wish they had booked.** See the [three-day itinerary](/guides/camiguin-itinerary).

**The mainland sand is black.** We say it in every guide because booking a volcanic island expecting powder-white sand is the one mistake that spoils a trip here. The white sand is offshore, at the sandbar.

## When the sea says no: the rainy-day plan

On a small island the weather cancels boats, and the visitors who cope best are the ones who arrived expecting it.

**What the rain does not touch.** [Ardent Hot Springs](/guides/camiguin-springs) is arguably better in cool, wet weather. Sto. Niño Cold Spring and the Bura soda pool are unaffected. The [waterfalls](/guides/camiguin-waterfalls) are at their heaviest and most impressive in the green season. The ring road is sealed and handles a downpour. The market happens regardless.

**What it does touch.** Every boat: the sandbar, Mantigue, the giant clams, every dive. The inland spurs out to Tuasan and some of the falls approaches get messy. And a scooter in a Camiguin afternoon downpour is a soaked hour rather than an adventure.

**The rule we give guests:** if your trip contains anything that floats, **keep one flexible day**. A three-day trip with a fixed sandbar morning and no spare day is a trip that can lose its best hour to a forecast. Our [month-by-month guide](/guides/best-time-to-visit-camiguin) says which weeks need that margin most.

If you would rather not manage any of this, that is most of what a small resort is for — [tell us your dates](/book) and the water, the transfer and the weather calls become somebody else's job.

**And ask somebody local rather than an app.** Island weather is hyper-local, and a radar picture of the Bohol Sea will not tell you whether the bancas are going out of Yumbing this morning. The people at your front desk will know by seven.

## Pasalubong: what to take home, and where to buy it

*Pasalubong* — the gift you bring back for the people who did not come — is a real institution here, and Camiguin has unusually good things to carry off it.

**Vjandep pastel.** The island's famous export: soft buns filled with yema custard, sold in boxes and bought by the armful in town and at the airport. This is what people on the mainland ask for by name when they hear you are going to Camiguin.

**Lanzones**, in season — roughly the second half of the year, peaking around the October [festival](/guides/camiguin-lanzones-festival). Buy from a roadside stall on the ring road rather than a shop: fresher, cheaper, and the money goes to the family that grew it.

**Kiping and the other local sweets**, plus dried fish if you are travelling with people who appreciate them. Our [what to eat guide](/guides/what-to-eat-in-camiguin) covers what each of these actually is.

**Woven and crafted work** from the island's artisans, including jewellery made from sea glass the tide brings in — the [community page](/community) names the partners we work with.

**Two practical notes.** Buy perishables on your last day rather than your first; the heat is not kind to custard. And the **public market in Mambajao is cheaper than the airport** for everything except the pastel, which costs about the same everywhere and is the one thing worth buying at the gate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are there ATMs in Camiguin?
Yes, a small number, concentrated around Mambajao, the capital on the north coast. They are reliable most of the time but can run out of cash after a weekend or a holiday and occasionally go offline. Withdraw in Cebu, Cagayan de Oro or Manila before flying in, ask for small denominations, and treat an island ATM as a backup rather than a plan.

### Do you need cash in Camiguin?
Yes, and more of it than feels sensible. Resorts and hotels take cards, but tricycle fares, boat fares, entrance fees, carinderias, market stalls and roadside sellers are all cash. A full day of the island's main attractions costs under ₱300 per person in fees, all of it paid in notes at gates.

### Can you drink the tap water in Camiguin?
No. Tap water is fine for showering and brushing teeth, but drinking water here is purified refill water, sold cheaply by the litre from refilling stations and provided by resorts, hotels and restaurants as standard. Bring a refillable bottle and fill it where you are staying.

### Which SIM card works in Camiguin?
Globe and Smart both cover the island, with reliable signal along the populated northern ring road and patchy coverage inland. Philippine law requires every prepaid SIM to be registered to an identity document — your passport, as a visitor — before it activates, so buy and register it at an official airport counter on the mainland, or use a travel eSIM instead.

### Is there Wi-Fi in Camiguin?
Yes, at resorts, hotels and the better restaurants, and it is good enough for messages, maps and a video call you do not mind dropping once. It is not reliable enough to build a working trip around on its own — bring mobile data as the backup if being online matters.

### What plug type and voltage does Camiguin use?
220 volts at 60 hertz, with flat two-pin sockets (type A), some type B with a round earth pin, and a few older round two-pin fittings. Visitors from the UK, Europe and Australia need an adapter. A power bank is worth packing — a day out here is long and there are no sockets at a waterfall.

### Is there a hospital in Camiguin?
Yes, the provincial hospital is in Mambajao, with pharmacies in town, and ordinary problems are well handled on the island. Anything serious means a flight to Cagayan de Oro or Cebu, which is why travel insurance that explicitly covers medical evacuation is the one thing we would not travel here without.

### What is closed on Sundays in Camiguin?
A good deal of ordinary commerce pauses on Sunday morning, when the island is at mass — Camiguin is deeply Catholic and the schedule shapes the day. Beaches, springs and waterfalls are unaffected. Plan Sunday mornings for the outdoors and errands for any other day.

### Do you tip in Camiguin?
It is appreciated rather than expected. Many restaurants, including ours, add a service charge of around 5% that goes to the team. Beyond that, rounding up a fare or leaving something for housekeeping is normal and welcome. Nobody will be offended either way.

### What language do they speak in Camiguin?
Bisaya, also called Cebuano, is the island's language, with Filipino and English both widely understood — you will have no practical difficulty anywhere. A few words still go a long way: *maayong buntag* (good morning), *salamat* (thank you), *pila?* (how much?).

### What should you bring to Camiguin that is hard to buy there?
Reef-safe sunscreen, insect repellent, any prescription medication in its original packaging with enough for the whole trip, rehydration salts, a power adapter, a power bank and a refillable water bottle. Specific brands may not be stocked on a small island, and the sun is stronger than most visitors expect.

### Where is the Camiguin tourism office?
The provincial tourism office is in Mambajao, and it is the right authority for current entrance fees, guide accreditation, permits for the Hibok-Hibok climb and the Lanzones Festival schedule. For anything where the number actually matters to your plans, confirm with them rather than with a travel article.
